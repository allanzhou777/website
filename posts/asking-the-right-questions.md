# The Hard Part Is Still Knowing What to Ask

*March 15, 2026 · note · #ai #tools #thought*

The other day, I was watching YouTube and came across a podcast with Nimit Sohoni, an AI researcher and former Citadel quant. He put it very bluntly: “90% of the battle in research is actually finding the right problems.” In the AI world, this feels very, very true. A single person can do incredible things with proper guidance.

Any agent today can one-shot many coding tasks. But it can also confidently—and sometimes perfectly—solve a problem that was never the one you needed answered. That is not a failure of the agent. It is a reminder that the hard part often happens before the prompt: deciding what matters, what constraint is real, and what would count as evidence that the work helped.

Research makes this obvious. A precise answer to an unimportant question is still not very useful. Sohoni's fuller point in [the conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_jECS37M3dQ) was that a problem has to be meaningful, interesting, and scoped tightly enough to make progress. Execution matters, but it matters after that.

The same thing is becoming obvious in software. AI can make implementation dramatically cheaper, which raises the value of judgment rather than removing it. The leverage is in understanding the system well enough to ask for the right change, and in checking whether the result is actually true.

I was struck by a similar line in [Lisa Su's 2026 MIT commencement address](https://news.mit.edu/2026/commencement-address-lisa-su-0528): “AI cannot decide which problems are worth solving.” It is a useful standard for building with these tools. The agent can help me move quickly; I still have to decide where I am going.
